# Adversarial Positioning Memo — NormTrace / "Legal Readiness" Op-Ed

**Role:** Tough peer reviewer / *Think Global Health* specialist editor
**Subject:** Op-ed claiming NormTrace (obligation-by-obligation mapping of domestic law vs. IHR (2005) requirements) adds finer-grained legal-readiness measurement than SPAR / JEE / GHS Index. Mexico case.
**Date of audit:** 1 July 2026
**Evidence rule applied:** Every citation below carries a real DOI/PMID retrieved and checked against PubMed metadata. Items I could not verify are flagged UNVERIFIED and are *not* to be presented as citeable.

---

## Bottom line up front

The piece is publishable, but the framing must be narrowed. The *concept* — that legislation is captured only as a coarse score by existing tools, that self-reports overstate capacity, and that domestic legal instruments are the real transmission belt of IHR obligations — is **not new**; it has been argued by Gostin's Legal Determinants of Health Commission, by Menon et al. on "Law and the JEE," and is implicit in the JEE's own P.1 technical area. What is plausibly novel is the **method**: a reproducible, obligation-level crosswalk from each IHR (2005) requirement to the specific domestic instrument that carries it, scored per-obligation rather than per-country. Sell the *instrument and the resolution*, not the *insight*. The Mexico "1985 Reglamento / perfect self-scores" example is the single strongest asset — lead with it, but present it as an illustrative probe (n=1, single-coder pilot), not as validated measurement.

## 1. Who has already said this (adjacent literature — all VERIFIED)

**On "legislation is a determinant, and law is the transmission belt of health capacity"**
- **Gostin et al. 2019**, *The legal determinants of health: harnessing the power of law for global health and sustainable development* (Lancet Commission). **PMID 31053306 · DOI 10.1016/S0140-6736(19)30233-8.** The foundational statement that domestic law is a first-order, under-measured determinant of health capacity. The op-ed's premise sits squarely downstream of this Commission; cite it or a reviewer will say the framing is uncredited.

**On "law inside the JEE — legislation as an IHR implementation lever" (closest conceptual competitor)**
- **Menon et al. 2018**, *Law and the JEE: Lessons for IHR Implementation*, Health Security. **PMID 30480502 · DOI 10.1089/hs.2018.0053.** Argues legal frameworks at national level are what operationalize IHR core capacities and connects this explicitly to the JEE. This is the paper closest to the op-ed's thesis and MUST be cited; the JEE technical area P.1 ("Legislation, Policy & Financing") is the incumbent the author is claiming to out-resolve.

**On SPAR-based capacity measurement (the tool the author critiques)**
- **Kandel et al. 2020**, *Health security capacities in the context of COVID-19 outbreak: an analysis of IHR annual report data from 182 countries*, Lancet. **PMID 32199075 · DOI 10.1016/S0140-6736(20)30553-5.** Built five indices from SPAR 2018 self-reports across 182 countries; the canonical demonstration of what SPAR *can* and *cannot* tell you. Cite as the state of the art the author is trying to refine.

**On pandemic-instrument governance & compliance (the treaty framing)**
- **Hoffman/Wenham et al. 2023**, *Increasing compliance with international pandemic law: international relations and new global health agreements*, Lancet. **PMID 37678291 · DOI 10.1016/S0140-6736(23)01527-1.** The "everyone signs, nobody implements" compliance-gap literature — directly relevant to the op-ed's implicit claim that a new agreement changes little without domestic legal internalization.
- **Eccleston-Turner & Rourke (Wenham grouping) 2023**, *Equity in the Pandemic Treaty: Access and Benefit-Sharing as a Policy Device or a Rhetorical Device?*, J Law Med Ethics. **PMID 37226758 · DOI 10.1017/jme.2023.59.** Grounds the PABS-annex point the op-ed makes.
- **Eccleston-Turner et al. 2025**, *The 2024 Amendments to the International Health Regulations: A New Era for Global Health Law in Pandemic Preparedness and Response?*, J Law Med Ethics. **PMID 40223587 · DOI 10.1017/jme.2025.16.** Current legal-scholarship take on the IHR amendment package; cite for the "still-unfinished legal architecture" claim.
- **2025**, *The Proposed Pandemic Agreement: A Pivotal Moment for Global Health Law*, J Law Med Ethics. **PMID 40221388 · DOI 10.1017/jme.2025.22.** The JLME 2025 legal-preparedness piece the brief asked about; use for the WHA78 Pandemic Agreement framing.

**On institutional fragmentation (why a single coarse legislation score persists)**
- **2023**, *Forum shifting in global health security*, Bull World Health Organ. **PMID 38313147 · DOI 10.2471/BLT.23.290480.** Supports the argument that legal obligations are scattered across regimes and never consolidated into one legible measure — the structural gap NormTrace claims to fill.

> **Note on "direct competitors":** I searched PubMed for peer-reviewed work performing *systematic obligation-by-obligation crosswalks of domestic legal instruments against each IHR (2005) requirement* and found none indexed. This is **absence of evidence, not proof of novelty** — comparable work may live in law reviews, WHO/O'Neill Institute grey literature, or the IHR legal-assessment toolkits (WHO's "IHR legislative assessment" guidance) that PubMed does not index. The author should search HeinOnline/SSRN and the O'Neill Institute output before claiming "first." What can be said safely: no *indexed biomedical* study does per-obligation domestic-law mapping at this resolution.

## 2. Two load-bearing claims — grounded

### (a) "Self-reported SPAR scores are inflated / diverge from external evaluation"

This is now **empirically supported**, and the best source is recent and specific:

- **[BEST] 2026**, *Estimating global public health security preparedness capacity: the contribution of SPAR and JEE*, Dialogues in Health. **PMID 41640577 · DOI 10.1016/j.dialog.2026.100282.** Compared **1,445 indicator-matched paired observations** from 108 SPAR and JEE assessments (93 States Parties, 2016–2023) with mixed-effects models. **Finding: SPAR (self-report) scores were higher than JEE (external) scores overall**, with least agreement at Level 3–4 indicators. **Caveat the author MUST NOT hide:** agreement *improved* in the 2022–2023 editions (25 of 28 matched indicators not significantly different). So the honest version is "self-reports have historically run above external evaluations, though the gap has narrowed recently" — not "self-reports are simply inflated."
- **Aitken et al. 2020**, *The Global Health Security Index and Joint External Evaluation score for health preparedness are not correlated with countries' COVID-19 detection response time and mortality outcome*, Epidemiol Infect. **PMID 32892793 · DOI 10.1017/S0950268820002046.** Supports the deeper point: even *external* preparedness scores failed to predict real pandemic performance — i.e., the problem is not only self-report bias but construct validity of the whole score family.
- **2026**, *A Review and Future Directions for Global Public Health Security Assessment Tools*, China CDC Weekly. **PMID 41550674 · DOI 10.46234/ccdcw2026.002.** States plainly that GHSI/JEE/SPAR scores "did not consistently align with countries' actual pandemic response performance." Good synthesis cite.

### (b) "US withdrawal from WHO was unexpected given the historical US leadership role"

- **[BEST] 2025**, *The United States withdrawal from the World Health Organization (WHO): its implications for global health governance*, Globalization and Health. **PMID 40796848 · DOI 10.1186/s12992-025-01137-0.** Peer-reviewed commentary that documents the **historically central US role — "a significant player... contributing substantial funding"** — and analyzes the Jan 2025 withdrawal. This is the citeable source for both halves of the claim (historical leadership + the rupture).
- **2026**, *U.S. withdrawal from WHO and aid programs: implications and opportunities for Africa's global health security*, Globalization and Health. **PMID 41639865 · DOI 10.1186/s12992-026-01189-w.** Corroborates the "long central role in global health" framing and the downstream funding shock. Use as second source.

> **Precision note:** the op-ed dates completion of withdrawal to 22 Jan 2026 and the WHO ~21% budget cut to the same period. The *executive order initiating* withdrawal is 20 Jan 2025 (per PMID 40796848); the 12-month notice period lands the *effective* exit in Jan 2026. Keep those two dates distinct or a reviewer will flag it. The specific "~21% budget cut" figure should be cited to a WHO programme-budget primary document, not to a commentary — I did not independently verify that percentage against a WHO source and it should be checked before print.

## 3. Likely critic objections and the strongest available response

| # | Hostile-reviewer objection | Strongest rebuttal / honest concession |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **"n = 1 country. This is an anecdote, not a measurement instrument."** | *Concede and reframe.* Mexico is a proof-of-concept probe, not a validation study. The contribution is the **method and codebook**, transferable to any State Party; the pilot demonstrates feasibility and surfaces a real discrepancy (1985 Reglamento vs. perfect self-scores). Do not claim measurement validity from one case — claim demonstrated feasibility and a testable hypothesis. |
| 2 | **"JEE technical area P.1 already scores Legislation, Policy & Financing. What does this add?"** | *Rebut on resolution.* P.1 collapses the entire legal domain into a handful of ordinal indicators scored 1–5 (Menon et al. 2018, PMID 30480502). NormTrace scores **per IHR obligation against the specific instrument that carries it** — a crosswalk, not a summary rating. The unit of analysis differs (obligation vs. country-capacity-area). That is the defensible delta. |
| 3 | **"Law-on-the-books ≠ implementation. A statute existing tells you nothing about enforcement."** | *Concede explicitly.* NormTrace measures *legal coverage/architecture*, not implementation or enforcement. This is a genuine limitation shared with all documentary legal analysis. Position it as a **necessary first layer** — you cannot implement an obligation no instrument carries — and cite the compliance-gap literature (Hoffman/Wenham 2023, PMID 37678291) as the reason the on-the-books layer is diagnostically useful even so. |
| 4 | **"Single coder. No inter-rater reliability. Coding is subjective."** | *Concede; commit to fix.* A single-coder pilot cannot report κ/agreement statistics. State this as a limitation and pre-register double-coding + a reliability statistic for the next tranche. The SPAR/JEE agreement paper (PMID 41640577) is the model: it quantifies agreement, and reviewers will expect the same discipline eventually. |
| 5 | **"Preliminary coding / not peer-reviewed / no published protocol."** | *Concede.* Frame as a pilot with a forthcoming protocol and open codebook. Reproducibility is the selling point — so publish the crosswalk template and coding rules as a supplement; that converts the weakness into the paper's differentiator. |
| 6 | **"The scores you attack don't predict outcomes anyway (GHSI/JEE failed in COVID). Why is a *finer* legal score better?"** | *Rebut on purpose, not prediction.* NormTrace is not offered as an outcome-predictor (Aitken 2020, PMID 32892793, shows none of these scores predict mortality). It is a **diagnostic of legal gaps** — it tells a ministry *which obligation lacks a domestic instrument*, which is actionable regardless of whether an aggregate index predicts mortality. Do not claim predictive validity; claim diagnostic actionability. |
| 7 | **"Mexico's self-reported 'perfect' legislation score may reflect a real newer instrument you missed, not a 1985 relic."** | *Pre-empt.* State the specific instruments reviewed and the search strategy. The claim hinges on having found *the* governing instrument; a reviewer with Mexican public-health-law knowledge will check. Cite the actual Reglamento and any amendments; if a post-1985 instrument exists, address why it doesn't discharge the obligation. |

## 4. The real contribution / gap (one paragraph)

**Yes, there is a genuine but narrow contribution.** The novelty is *not* the insight that legislation is under-measured (Gostin 2019, PMID 31053306), nor that self-reports overstate capacity (now empirically shown by PMID 41640577), nor that law matters to IHR implementation (Menon 2018, PMID 30480502) — all of that is established and must be cited rather than reintroduced. The defensible contribution is **methodological resolution**: a reproducible obligation-by-obligation crosswalk that maps each specific IHR (2005) requirement to the specific domestic legal instrument that carries (or fails to carry) it, scored at the obligation level rather than as one collapsed country-capacity rating. No *indexed biomedical* study does this at that granularity, and existing tools (SPAR self-report, JEE P.1, GHS Index) structurally cannot, because their unit of analysis is the capacity area, not the obligation. **What to NOT overclaim:** (1) do not call it "the first" legal-mapping effort without checking law-review/SSRN/WHO-toolkit grey literature; (2) do not present n=1 single-coder pilot output as validated measurement; (3) do not imply it measures implementation or predicts outcomes — it measures legal coverage on the books, a necessary but insufficient first layer. Framed as *"a finer-grained, reproducible diagnostic of domestic legal coverage against IHR obligations, piloted on Mexico,"* it is a real and publishable contribution. Framed as *"a new measure of legal readiness that improves on SPAR/JEE/GHS,"* it overreaches on validity it has not yet earned.
